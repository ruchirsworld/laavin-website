Landscaping for Office Premises: A Practical Guide

Laavin Infrascapes10 Oct 20266 min read

An office's landscape is the first thing visitors see and one of the few parts of the building people choose to spend time in. It also costs money every month to water and maintain. Good office landscaping balances the two: spaces people actually use, built from plants and systems that are affordable to keep alive.

Start with how people use the site

Walk the site with the facility team and map how people move and where they stop: the entrance and drop-off, walkways between buildings, parking, break and smoking areas, places people take calls or eat lunch, and the views from desks and meeting rooms. Put the best landscaping where people are. Low-traffic edges and service areas can stay simple and cheap to maintain.

Shade comes first

In an Indian summer, shade is the most valued feature of any outdoor space. Plant shade trees over parking, along walkways and around seating. Where trees will take years to grow, pergolas and shade structures fill the gap.

Choose plants for the site, not the brochure

  • Favour native and well-adapted species. They need less water, fewer chemicals and less replacement.
  • Group plants by water need so thirsty and drought-tolerant plants aren't on the same watering schedule.
  • Keep lawns where people use them. Lawns are the thirstiest part of a landscape; use ground covers and shrub beds elsewhere.
  • Plan for the full-grown size so trees don't crowd buildings, block signage or lift paving in five years.

Water: the biggest running cost

Use drip irrigation for shrub beds, hedges and trees, and sprinklers on timers for lawns. If the campus has a sewage treatment plant, treated water can often be used for irrigation with proper filtration. Rainwater harvesting and recharge pits reduce runoff and top up groundwater. Our guide to drip irrigation for large campuses covers where each system pays off.

Terrace gardens and green walls

Terrace gardens turn unused roofs into breakout space and help insulate the floor below. They need proper waterproofing, a root barrier, a drainage layer and lightweight soil, and your structural engineer should confirm the roof can carry the load before design starts. Green walls look striking in lobbies and on facades, but they depend on built-in irrigation and regular care, so budget for maintenance from the start.

Green building ratings

Landscaping can contribute to green building ratings such as IGBC and GRIHA through native planting, limited lawn area, efficient irrigation and shade that reduces the heat island effect. If your building is pursuing a rating, bring the landscape contractor in early so the design supports the credits you are targeting.

A design your team can't maintain will start declining within a year. Agree the maintenance scope at the design stage, not after handover.

Maintenance is where landscapes succeed or fail

Every landscape needs scheduled pruning, mowing, fertilising, pest control and irrigation checks. Agree who does what, how often, and how plant failures are replaced. A simple monthly report with photographs keeps everyone honest.

Checklist for facility managers

  • Site plan and photographs of the current condition.
  • Where people walk, park, sit and gather.
  • Water source and quality: municipal supply, borewell or treated STP water.
  • Roof loads and waterproofing status, if terraces are in scope.
  • Green building rating targets, if any.
  • Budget for both construction and the first year of maintenance.
