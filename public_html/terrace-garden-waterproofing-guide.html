Terrace and Balcony Gardens: What to Check Before You Plant

Laavin Infrascapes11 Oct 20266 min read

A terrace garden can cool the floor below, give people a place to sit and turn a hot, empty roof into the best space in the building. It can also leak, overload the slab or die in its first summer. The difference is almost always decided before a single plant goes in.

1. Start with the structure

Wet soil is heavy, and a roof slab was usually designed for people and water tanks, not for a garden. Before anything else, have a structural engineer confirm how much load the terrace can safely carry. Plan heavier elements, such as deep planters, trees and water features, over beams and columns rather than in the middle of a span.

2. Fix the waterproofing first

Most terrace garden problems are really waterproofing problems. Inspect the existing membrane, repair or replace it, and add a root barrier so roots cannot work their way into joints. A protection layer over the membrane stops it being punctured during installation.

Then test it. A simple ponding test, where the roof is flooded and checked for leaks over a day or two, is far cheaper than lifting a finished garden to find a leak.

Waterproofing is the one part of a terrace garden you cannot easily redo later. Get it right, and test it, before you plant.

3. Plan the drainage

Water must leave the roof quickly, especially in the monsoon. The usual build-up is a drainage layer over the waterproofing, a filter fabric to keep soil out of it, and then the growing medium. The roof should slope towards outlets, and every outlet should stay reachable for cleaning.

4. Use a lightweight growing mix

Ordinary garden soil is too heavy and compacts in containers. Terrace gardens use a lighter mix, typically built from cocopeat, compost and a mineral such as perlite or vermiculite, which holds water without becoming waterlogged. Depth depends on what you plant: lawns and ground covers need the least, shrubs need more, and small trees need the deepest beds or large planters.

5. Choose plants for heat and wind

A Delhi rooftop is hotter, windier and brighter than the ground below. Plants that cope well include bougainvillea, frangipani, hibiscus, tecoma, ornamental grasses and many succulents. Herbs such as lemongrass and tulsi do well in containers. Keep tall or top-heavy plants away from the edges, and anchor large planters against wind.

  • Full sun and wind: bougainvillea, ornamental grasses, succulents.
  • Flowering colour: hibiscus, tecoma, frangipani in larger planters.
  • Shade and screening: pergolas with climbers rather than heavy trees.

6. Water with drip, on a timer

Rooftop plants dry out fast in May and June and need far less water in winter. A drip system on a timer, with a separate line for any lawn, delivers water at the roots and can be adjusted by season. It also keeps water off the waterproofing where it is not needed.

7. Look after it

Clear every outlet before the monsoon, trim plants that catch the wind, top up the growing mix as it settles, and feed regularly, because containers hold fewer nutrients than open ground.

8. Balconies follow the same rules

A balcony garden is a terrace garden in miniature. Check what the slab can carry, raise planters on feet or trays so water can drain off the floor, use the same lightweight mix, and choose plants for the light the balcony actually gets. Railing planters must be fixed securely, especially on higher floors.
